RESEARCH PROJECT

A Shared but Individually Scaled EEG Signature of Meditation

Why EEG-based meditation monitors struggle with new people, and what it takes to fix it[cite: 5].

THE PROBLEM

Background & Problem Statement

Electroencephalography (EEG) records the brain's tiny electrical signals through sensors on the scalp, forming rhythms at different speeds (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma) that shift with mental states[cite: 5]. Meditation is a private mental state; researchers rely on instructions or self-report to know when it happens[cite: 5]. EEG could track it objectively, but classifiers that separate meditation from mind wandering work well on the person they were trained on and only modestly on anyone new[cite: 5]. A practical monitor can't be retrained for every user[cite: 5]. So we ask why transfer fails, and test two explanations[cite: 5].

EXPLANATION 01

Individual Signature

Everyone's brain changes in its own way[cite: 5]. Nothing transfers between people[cite: 5].

EXPLANATION 02

Shared Signature, Individual Scale

Brains change in a common direction, but by different amounts[cite: 5].

RESEARCH QUESTION

Our Core Question

Is meditation's EEG signal specific to each individual, or shared but too faint to read reliably across people[cite: 5]?

METHODOLOGY

Our Approach

We process raw EEG scalp recordings from two open datasets through one shared clean-up (same filters, re-referencing, and eye/muscle artifact removal for everyone) to extract brain-rhythm features (share of power in each frequency band, per electrode)[cite: 5].

DATASET A

Block Design

Practitioners of three traditions (Himalayan Yoga, Vipassana, Isha Shoonya) and non-meditators alternate between meditating and deliberately letting thoughts wander[cite: 5].

DATASET B

Experience Sampling

Meditators are interrupted at random moments and report whether they were meditating or mind wandering[cite: 5].

Key Questions Evaluated

Q1

Direction

Does the same rhythm change the same way in most people[cite: 5]?

Q2

Transfer

Does a model trained on others recognise meditation in a new person[cite: 5]?

Q3

Personalise

How much of a new person's own data does it take to adapt[cite: 5]?

RESULTS

What We're Finding

FINDING 01

A Shared Direction

Theta rhythm power rises during meditation in most people, across traditions and both datasets, and in non-meditators focusing on the breathing techniques[cite: 5]. It reflects the meditative state, not years of practice[cite: 5].

FINDING 02

A Genuine Brain Rhythm

The increase survives separating true oscillations from the spectrum's background slope[cite: 5].

FINDING 03

An Individual Size

The shift is small and varies widely between people[cite: 5]. A one-size-fits-all model works above chance but modestly, levels off as more people are added, and succeeds mostly for those with a strong shift[cite: 5].

FINDING 04

Calibration Helps, at a Price

A person's own data improves the model, but much of the early gain is learning the person; meditation-specific gains need minutes, not seconds[cite: 5]. We combine a population model learned from everyone else with a few minutes of the new person's EEG to create a personalised model[cite: 5].

CONCLUSION

Takeaway

Meditation leaves a shared EEG signature whose strength is specific to the individual subject[cite: 5]. Practical monitoring should pair a common model with short personal calibration[cite: 5].
